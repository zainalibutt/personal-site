A question this site keeps returning to, this time at a card table: how do you
know a record is true? Proof-Lens asks it of a file, Melody of a filing, Revenue
OS of a sent email. River asks it of a shuffle, and of every chip that moves.

**This is an early v1, live since late September.** What you can play today is
the 2D table. The 3D venues, built in Blender, are in development, and the
render above is one of them. The repository is private.

## The problem

Online poker is either a mobile casino engineered to sell chips, or a console
title that wants a full evening, a headset and a lobby queue. Neither lets a
group of friends open one link and be dealt into the same hand.

And every online card game asks for the same leap of faith: that the server
shuffled honestly. You never see the deck. You are told it was random.

## The approach

The server owns everything, and proves what it can.

- **The rules are a pure engine.** Every rule of Texas Hold'em lives in one
  package with no network, no database and no clock. It is the most heavily
  tested code in the project, part of more than 1,500 tests that run on every
  push against Node 22 and 24.
- **The server owns every chip.** Clients send intentions and the server
  decides. Chips move only through an append-only ledger, so a balance is the
  sum of its history rather than a number someone can overwrite.
- **Shuffles are committed before the deal.** Each hand starts from a fresh
  32-byte server seed. Its hash is published before any player's seed is known,
  the deck is drawn from all of them together, and the seed is revealed after
  the hand settles. Any player can check that the deck they were dealt was the
  one promised.
- **No real money, by construction.** Chips cannot be bought or cashed out, and
  no code path may create a purchase route. That keeps River out of a regulatory
  category it has no business being in.

## The bots

Empty seats fill with bots, so a group can always get a game. They have
ordinary names, and nothing at the table says which is strong.

A bot sees only what its seat could legally know. It never sees another
player's cards or the cards still to come, and the server turns every decision
into a legal move. Those rules came first, before any attempt to make the bots
good.

The first bots were rule-based, and lost heads-up to every test style by 76 to
101 big blinds per 100 hands. Each version since has been measured rather than
judged by eye. The live champion, Nell, runs a CFR+ solver on every two-player
river, solving from both players' ranges, then bends the solution towards what
each opponent tends to do, trusting a read only as far as the evidence for it
goes. Across twelve fresh tables that beat the previous version by 24.5 big
blinds per 100 hands. Against Slumbot, a competition-grade heads-up bot, she
comes out about break-even over 7,231 hands: 4.5 big blinds per 100 behind, with
all-ins valued at their odds. The interval runs from 45 behind to 36 ahead, so
she may still be losing, and the claim is break-even, not better.

## Learning the turn

Nell solves only the river exactly, because nothing can solve a whole hand of
poker while it is being played. DeepStack, one of the research bots that beat
professional players, got around that with neural networks that estimate what
the rest of a hand is worth, so the solver can stop early and ask. That is the
research half of River.

I trained value networks in PyTorch on solver output, starting from 200,600
river spots solved overnight, and built a depth-limited turn solver that uses a
network to value everything after the turn. The turn network's error fell from
5.7% to 3.3% of the pot, measured against turns solved exactly over every river
card, once it learned what the betting adds rather than only what the cards are
worth.

The turn solver made the bot harder to exploit: a best-response opponent that
knows its strategy won 42% less from it. It still lost chips to Nell at the
tables. So I instrumented every hand and built an explainer for each turn
decision. One fault fell out quickly, a broken handover of ranges from the turn
to the river. The rest traced to one hand: the bot folding jacks full. Re-solved
with exact values it still folded, because a subgame solved on its own, against
an opponent assumed to play perfectly, is unsafe. That is a known failure in the
research literature, and naming it decided the next step instead of another
round of tuning.

**This part is research.** The bot at the live tables does not use a network
yet.

## What was hard

Discovering that "provably fair" was a claim the first version could not back.

The original shuffle drew every hand in a room from one seed, chosen when the
room was created, using a generator with about four billion possible states. A
deck of 52 cards has far more orderings than that, so most hands could never be
dealt at all. The commitment proved the server had not changed its mind. It
proved nothing about how the seed was chosen. The rule that came out of it is in
the decision log as _provably fair, or do not claim it_.

The other hard part was the characters. More than thirty passes in Blender took
the first one from a stock suit to a tailored black-tie figure, but none
produced a seated poker pose that held up, so a character's pose and fit are now
made by hand. The venues themselves stay as code: geometry, light, cameras and
props all live in a Python pipeline, and building is the only way a venue comes
into existence.

## What I'd change

Next is safe continual re-solving: solving each street again from where the
hand actually stands, in a way that cannot be exploited the way the turn solver
was, from the flop down, with networks for the flop and before it. It goes live
only when it beats Nell head-to-head on the same deals.

And the 3D venues need to reach the live table. Until they do, the strongest
visual work in River is something players cannot yet see.

## Method

I design River, set the rules its work is held to, and verify what ships. AI
coding agents do much of the implementation under that review, and the record of
it, including what went wrong, is kept beside the code.
